About me

I’m a 5th year Ph.D candidate in the Computer Science and Engineering at the University of Michigan. My research focuses on human-AI interactions, with a specific focus on how end-user engage with AI systems, particularly when they lack expertise and AI literacy. I am working on developing a contestable design framework for AI systems, which aims to help improve design mechanisms to enable end-users to critically assess and contest AI.

Project One

Evaluating Text-Entry Experience for Multilingual Users

Problem statement

Devices are designed with a default English interface and lack multilingual support for text-entry. We envision to bridge the parity of experience between the multilingual and English text-based communicators.

Solution statement

We identify usability issues for our users, brainstorm solutions, and conduct user studies on our prototype. We found breakdowns in switching between keyboards, autocorrect interruptions, and unhelpful text suggestions in context of text-messaging. We propose the creation of keyboard profiles and other autocorrect assistive tools for multilingual users, with qualitative testing for usability issue.

Design Process

  1. 01User research surveyDesigned and administered a survey across a diverse range of students. This survey created awareness of the current state of the tools within niche of multi language use in text-entry and provide direction for future research.
  2. 02User research interviewsContextual inquiries were conducted using adept users at multilingual communication, especially with mobile texting methods. These inquiries led to various key similarities in breakdowns such as interruptions with autocorrect, problems with switching and differentiating between various keyboard types, and unhelpful text suggestions.
  3. 03Solution ideationsUsing the findings, relevant user requirements were developed that met and inform many possible solutions. Developed user requirements and functional constraints.
  4. 04PrototypingLow Fidelity: Sketches, Storyboards, Paper prototype. Usability evaluation. High fidelity prototype.

User Research: Survey

The survey was conducted using Google forms and was generally on the topic of auto-correct and text suggestions.

We received 61 response for our survey questions.

Contextual Inquiries

The contextual interviews were conducted through virtual and in-person meetings with the participants in private and familiar settings such as the participants’ residences to restrict external influences on the participants’ responses. Responses and clarified interpretations from the participants were recorded and used to construct sequence, and flow diagrams.

User Requirements

Users must be able to identify the language mode of the multilingual text entry method before starting to communicate in any text messaging app.

Users must be aware of changes made by autocorrect so they can recognize autocorrect errors while drafting a text message in any language.

Users must be able to use text assistive features (text suggestions, autocorrect, etc.) to make messaging more error-less to reduce user frustration when communicating in a non-English language.

User Evaluation

The methods used in this quantitative user evaluation make use of two studies which include a subjective rating of user satisfaction with the design and a standard transcription task empirical study.

Phase 1

Have participants use the keyboard settings page on Figma and ask how them to rate the design, clarity and the ease of navigation of the prototype using Likert scale

Phase 2

Have participants send a non-English message using the English keyboard and then using the new default keyboard they selected in phase 1

For each scenario, measure the number of times the participant corrected an autocorrect suggestion as well as the number of errors that remained when they sent the final message

Use a Wilcoxon test to compare the error rates between English and non-English keyboards

Project Two

AI-enhanced Closed-loop Communication Annotation Tool for Medical Education

Background

Closed-Loop Communication (CLC) is important in the medical field for preventing communication errors and is frequently evaluated as a critical skill during medical training.

“In closed-loop communication, the person receiving instruction or information repeats it back to make sure the message is understood correctly, and the sender confirms to “close the loop.” It does not require more time, and in fact, it is likely to save time. But ultimately, it's an important tool that protects our patients from communication errors that can lead to serious consequences.”

— American Hospital Association

A: John, could you get 20 ml saline solution?

Motivation

While practicing CLC is important for training medical students, instructors have to rely on their memory to evaluate their performance, without quantitative metrics and records of specific instances for improvements.

Manually labeling all transcribed training recordings requires medical expertise and is too expensive and time-consuming.

We hope to assist medical instructors by creating a tool for evaluating the use of closed-loop communication to improve the students’ communication skills and prevent miscommunications in emergency situations.

Integrate AI-technology to assist medical professionals using our annotation tool.

Contextual Inquiry: User Requirements

  • Users must be able to understand the definition of CLC in medical context.
  • Users must be able to distinguish between a request and a response in a relevant conversation.
  • Users must be able to recognize unresponded requests.
  • Users must be able to identify which part of the annotation tool is assisted by AI.

Design for Manual and AI Interface

  • Present the transcript (AI-generated) clearly along with the video of the VR training session for more context
  • Distinguish between instances of CLC and unresponded questions or requests
  • Intuitively modify and add new CLC pairs and unresponded instances to the transcript

Project Three

Assessing End-user Ability to Verify and Contest Adverse AI Decisions and Seek Recourse from Negative Consequences

Ability to Verify and Contest AI Decision

The ability of end-users to effectively verify AI decisions and contest and appeal undesirable AI outcomes could reduce the risk of harm when interacting with AI.

Verify

Validating the correctness and desirability of AI’s Recommendation.

Fok and Weld et al 2024. In Search of Verifiability: Explanations Rarely Enable Complementary Performance in AI-Advised Decision Making. arXiv:2305.07722
Contest

Reject or oppose wrong or unfavorable decisions from AI.

Venkatasubramanian et al 2020. The philosophical basis of algorithmic recourse. In FAT’20. https://doi.org/10.1145/3351095.3372876
Seek Recourse

Appeal wrong or unfavorable outcome to reverse the AI decision.

Kluttz et al 2022. Shaping our tools: Contestability as a means to promote responsible algorithmic decision making in the professions. In Ethics of Data and Analytics.

How do you Verify AI Recommendations?

Problem Statement

Designing AI that is open to Verification, Contestation, and recours does not mean that the end-user will be able to verify, contest and seek recourse on their own.

Why is that challenging to Verify and contest AI?

Verifying and contesting AI Requires task expertise.

Challenges In Verifying And Contesting AI

End-users who are lack AI literacy may not be able to verify, let alone contest AI.

Even when they have task expertise, they may be misled by AI’s incorrect recommendations.

Salimzadeh, et al. 2023. A Missing Piece in the Puzzle: Considering the Role of Task Complexity in Human-AI Decision Making. In UMAP’23. https://doi.org/10.1145/3565472.3592959

Research Questions

We investigated if simply opening AI to contestation and recourse is enough to empower end-users to detect and counter untrustworthy AI.

  1. RQ1Can end-users, who lack task expertise and AI literacy, verify the quality and desirability of AI and its decisions?
  2. RQ2If and when do such end-users choose to contest AI decisions and outcomes of those decisions?
  3. RQ3Do such end-users seek recourse after unfavourable outcomes when using AI?

Hypothesis

End-users who lack task expertise and AI literacy cannot verify the quality and desirability of AI decisions and thus do not know when to contest and seek recourse.

We conducted empirical user study followed by quantitative analysis method.

Experiment Design

We develop an experiment to conduct user study.

We focus on AI that acts as a decision support(DSS) and recommends actions to aid end-users to achieve well defined goals.

End-users can then choose to rely on AI advice to make their decisions.

Findings

Our findings showed that end-users who lack task expertise and AI literacy cannot effectively verify and contest AI decisions on their own.

Responsibility To End-users

Delegating responsibility to verify AI to end-users is detrimental to their ability to contest and reverse unfavorable outcomes.

End-users might not even be aware that the AI is the cause of their unfavorable outcomes.

Conclusion

Our findings call for the design solution that will focus on raising end-user AI literacy and task expertise to increase their ability to critically reflect on and verify AI.
